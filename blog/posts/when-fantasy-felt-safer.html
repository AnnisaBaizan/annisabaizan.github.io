When Fantasy Felt Safer Than Reality

On workplace exclusion, manufactured happiness, and the mind's desperate attempt to meet legitimate needs

📅 February 2026 ⏱️ 10 min read 🏷️ Maladaptive Daydreaming, Belonging, Coping

When I look back at that workplace, I see a story I didn't want to admit I was living. A story about what happens when the mind tries to solve a problem the world won't fix.

✦ ✦ ✦

I. The Quiet Denial

At first, I fell into denial — not the loud kind, but the quiet, protective type. I convinced myself the exclusion wasn't intentional, just an adjustment phase. They were already a tight group. I was new. It would take time.

So I compensated. I overextended. I overfunctioned. I kept giving — emotional labor, knowledge, even material support — thinking that if I just proved my worth clearly enough, they would eventually let me in.

But when nothing changed, the denial collapsed.

✦ ✦ ✦

II. When Denial Becomes Self-Blame

The classic internal attribution kicked in: Maybe I was the problem. Maybe I was too much. Too intense. Too different.

On Internal Attribution Bias

Social psychology identifies "internal attribution bias" — our tendency to blame ourselves for negative social outcomes while attributing others' behavior to external circumstances. When excluded, we assume it's because of who we are, not because of group dynamics or others' limitations.

This bias protects us with the illusion of control: if it's our fault, we can fix it. But it also erodes self-worth when the "fixing" never works.

It became a loop of intrusive self-questioning that slowly eroded my sense of self. After that came anger — After everything I gave, this is the return?

But the part that really swallowed me was what came next.

✦ ✦ ✦

III. The Fantasies Begin

The maladaptive daydreaming didn't start suddenly. It slid into my mind quietly.

At first, it was just harmless imagination — small scenes where they included me in lunch plans or asked me to join their conversations. Nothing dramatic. Just the social warmth I never got in reality.

Over time, the fantasies became more structured, almost like little story arcs.

I'd imagine scenarios where one of them finally noticed how much I contributed and said something like, "Hey, join us. We didn't mean to leave you out." Sometimes I'd imagine a whole scene during a meeting where someone openly appreciated my work, and the rest began treating me differently afterward.

✦ ✦ ✦

IV. Deeper Immersion

They got more immersive.

I created "ideal versions" of my coworkers — versions who respected me, joked with me, waited for me before going out. In these scenes, I wasn't invisible. I was part of the group. I belonged.

There were days I replayed the same scenario three, four, maybe six times. Different angles. Different dialogue. I'd imagine myself walking into the pantry and someone turning around with a smile — "Oh, we were just talking about you. Come sit with us."

Sometimes it got more dramatic — someone defending me, standing up for me, suddenly realizing they misjudged me. It became a kind of emotional compensation, a place where my unmet needs finally had somewhere to land.

On Maladaptive Daydreaming Disorder

Psychologist Eli Somer first identified Maladaptive Daydreaming Disorder (MDD) in 2002. Unlike normal daydreaming, MDD is extensive, vivid fantasy activity that replaces human interaction and interferes with daily functioning. It often develops as a coping mechanism for loneliness, trauma, or unmet attachment needs.

The brain treats these imagined social interactions as real enough to trigger emotional responses — temporary relief from loneliness, followed by deeper emptiness when reality returns.

✦ ✦ ✦

V. When Fantasy Becomes Comfort

The worst part was when the fantasies started to feel more comforting than reality.

I'd lose minutes — sometimes almost an hour — drifting through them, especially when real life felt too cold or too empty. It was like my brain was trying to build the connection I couldn't find outside. And slowly, they became a loop. A habit. A place I kept going back to even when I knew it wasn't helping.

The mind will always try to meet its needs. When reality won't provide safety, connection, or belonging, the brain manufactures them internally. This is not weakness — this is survival.

Trauma Psychology Principle

And when the daydreams became more vivid than the real interactions I had with these people, that's when I knew something was off.

It took three months until I realized it.

✦ ✦ ✦

VI. The Realization

My emotional attachment was being built on imaginary versions of them, not the real ones.

That's what finally pushed me to reset — dropping all attachment levels back to zero. No attachment. No emotional investment. No imagined closeness. No expectations.

On Manufactured Connection & Reality Testing

When fantasy becomes preferable to reality, it signals that our attachment system perceives the external environment as unsafe. The brain creates an internal "secure base" through imagination because the real world won't provide one.

Recovery requires what therapists call "reality testing" — consciously distinguishing between what is real and what is imagined, then grieving the gap between them.

✦ ✦ ✦

VII. The Reset

And strangely, that reset made things clearer.

I realized most of the pain came from the expectations I built, not from the people themselves. They were just being themselves — a group that had formed before I arrived, with dynamics I was never going to penetrate no matter how hard I tried.

Letting that go gave me space to breathe again. To detach. To see the situation without the emotional fog.

Acceptance is not defeat. It is the courageous acknowledgment of what is, which frees us to respond wisely rather than react desperately.

Radical Acceptance (Tara Brach)

The fantasies didn't disappear immediately. But they lost their grip. I stopped needing them to feel okay.

✦ ✦ ✦

Epilogue: What I Learned

Looking back, I don't regret the daydreaming. It kept me functional when I couldn't fix the real problem. It gave me a place to land when nowhere else felt safe.

But I also learned this: the mind is incredibly resourceful, but it cannot replace reality indefinitely. Fantasy can be a bridge, but it cannot be a destination.

On Healthy vs. Maladaptive Coping

All coping mechanisms exist on a spectrum. Daydreaming becomes maladaptive when it prevents us from addressing real problems or building real connections. It becomes adaptive when it provides temporary relief while we gather resources to change our situation.

The key question: Is this coping mechanism helping me survive until I can change things, or is it preventing me from trying?

• • •

If you've ever found yourself building entire worlds in your head because the real one wouldn't let you in — you're not broken. You're human. Your brain was doing exactly what it's designed to do: find safety, even if it has to create it.

The hard part is knowing when to let those worlds go. When to stop feeding the fantasy and start accepting the reality. When to grieve what you wished for and build something new with what actually is.

That's where I am now. Not in the fantasy. Not in the pain. Somewhere in between, learning to exist without needing to be seen by people who were never going to look.
