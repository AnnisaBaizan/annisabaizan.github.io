Personal Essay

On Letting One Thing Go

while keeping everything else

📅 February 2026 ⏱️ 12 min read 🏷️ Attachment, Growth, Letting Go

Listen twice: first to what's being said, second to who said it.

John Sherman Cooper

There are feelings that arrive without names, questions that linger without answers, and hearts that choose to stay even when leaving would be easier. This is a story about all three.

✦ ✦ ✦

I. The Beginning of Wondering

I do not remember the exact moment it started. Perhaps it was never a moment at all — perhaps it was a slow accumulation of small things: the way they explained ideas I could not grasp, the patience hidden beneath sharp words, the strange brightness in them that they never seemed to notice themselves.

They became my teacher without intending to. My mentor without asking. My devil's advocate when I needed someone to challenge me. My harsh guardian when I was too soft on myself. My best friend when I needed someone to stay.

And somewhere along the way, without permission, they became something I cannot name.

On Attachment Theory

Psychologist John Bowlby's attachment theory suggests we form deep emotional bonds not through dramatic moments, but through consistent patterns of interaction. The accumulation of small gestures — a patient explanation, a challenging question, a presence during difficulty — creates invisible threads stronger than conscious intention.

We don't choose who becomes important to us. The heart makes these decisions in the quiet spaces between words.

From the beginning, I knew I wasn't someone special to them. They said their tone was simply their "natural way," and I believed them. But I also saw how gently they could treat those who truly mattered. I understood that difference. I accepted it with a quiet smile, as if humor could soften the sting.

Still, my heart learned to hold them gently.

• • •

Lately I have been wondering what this feeling truly is. Is it love, or care, or empathy — or something softer that only the universe understands? Because whatever name I give it, it keeps returning to them.

If wanting someone to be happy is love — then what is this?

If praying for someone's success without them ever hearing — is that love?

If feeling frustrated when they speak harshly about themselves — what do I call this?

I am afraid to hurt them, so I shape my words softer than I feel them. Not because I want to hide, but because I care about their peace more than my own urgency.

Is that not something close to love?

✦ ✦ ✦

II. The Weight of Small Things

I do not think they are cruel. They are simply human, and my pain comes mostly from the fragile places inside me — although not everything is insecurity. Some things truly hurt.

There is a word they use sometimes — and every time it lands, the walls I have spent years building crumble in an instant. They do not know this, but that word touches the one value I fought so hard to build.

I was not born with the shield of beauty that protects so many others. I cannot change my face, so I changed my path instead. I worked harder than I should have, bled longer than anyone knew, climbed with weary hands and breaking bones just to stand where others arrive with ease.

This is the only strength I have built to hold my worth. And when it is shattered by someone whose opinion matters — it hurts in ways I cannot explain.

On Self-Worth & Defensive Mechanisms

Psychologists identify "compensatory achievement" as a defense mechanism where we build competence in one area to counterbalance perceived deficits in another. When someone threatens this carefully constructed pillar of self-worth, the psychological impact resembles what researchers call "ego depletion" — a sudden draining of our emotional resources.

The words that hurt most are not random. They target the foundations we've worked hardest to build.

• • •

They often teach me without warning — not because I ask, but because that is who they are. And I accept it because I like learning from them. But when frustration appears if I fail to understand, everything in me becomes quiet.

It is one of the reasons I hesitate to ask them anything. I do not want to feel small next to them. I do not want to feel like a burden.

They once said, "I expect talking to you feels calm." Yet too often I feel read through shadows, my meaning slipping away before it reaches them. That is why I whisper, not demand. That is why my words come wrapped in caution, my voice restrained by fear.

Why do I still stay, despite all of this?

Why does my heart refuse to leave?

✦ ✦ ✦

III. The Place You Land

Ironically, I am almost always there whenever their heart is broken. I am the one who stays when the world feels too heavy. I am the one who listens when the silence becomes unbearable.

Maybe I am a place they land — but never the place they stay.

On "Safe Haven" vs "Secure Base" in Attachment

Attachment research distinguishes between two relationship functions: a "safe haven" (someone you turn to in distress) and a "secure base" (someone whose presence enables exploration and growth). Healthy relationships provide both.

When someone uses you only as a safe haven — seeking comfort during crisis but disappearing once stabilized — it creates what researchers call "one-sided attachment labor." You become emotional infrastructure, not an equal partner.

That is why the jealousy comes — not because I want to control them, but because I wish our presence to each other mattered in the same measure. They are close enough to make me stay, but at the same time, distant enough to never choose me.

The cruelest thing about intermittent reinforcement is that it works. Uncertainty creates stronger bonds than consistency ever could.

Behavioral Psychology Principle

Sometimes it feels like they cherish the comfort I bring more than the person I am. Sometimes I wonder if they fear losing me — but never fear hurting me.

But maybe that is only my fear echoing back to me.

• • •

Their name is still loud in my head. Close enough to keep me waiting, yet far enough to leave me unsure. It becomes heavier by day. Louder by night.

I see them there in silence, and I make scenarios of them here with me. Yes, I used to create a manufactured happiness — but thank you for letting me see you. Thank you for this strange gift of knowing you exist.

On Limerence & Obsessive Thinking

Psychologist Dorothy Tennov coined the term "limerence" to describe involuntary romantic obsession characterized by intrusive thinking, fear of rejection, and physical symptoms of anxiety. The brain treats this state similarly to addiction — the person becomes a source of dopamine hits through mere thoughts of them.

Limerence intensifies when reciprocation is uncertain. The "maybe" keeps the mind locked in a loop of hope and fear, creating the psychological experience of depth and intensity.

Why is this so bold, so thick, so deep?

✦ ✦ ✦

IV. The Quiet Transformation

If the quiet effort to become a better version of myself — is that not love?

Because without them asking, their presence made me want to grow. I learned things I once ignored. I sharpened my mind. I tried to understand their logic, their worldview, their brilliance — so I could speak with them from a place of balance, not confusion.

If becoming better not to be chosen, but to meet them with dignity — is that not love?

On Intrinsic Motivation & Personal Growth

Psychologists distinguish between extrinsic motivation (changing to gain external rewards) and intrinsic motivation (changing because the growth itself feels meaningful). When we transform ourselves not to win someone's affection, but to stand alongside them as equals — that's intrinsic growth sparked by external inspiration.

The most profound transformations happen when we change not to be loved, but to become worthy of our own respect.

They taught me patience I did not know I had. They showed me the gaps in my thinking I never wanted to see. They challenged me in ways that made me uncomfortable, then grateful, then stronger.

I am sorry if I cannot immediately understand the way they think. They need to explain their thoughts, explain themselves. But one thing they must know — I am willing to understand. I am willing to hear their explanation. It does not have to be all at once if they are tired of explaining. We can take it slowly. I am sorry if I am slow to grasp or if I misunderstand.

I am still learning them.

• • •

The feeling I feel towards them is so strong, so deep. This feeling — like a luxury thing. So pure, so sincere. They are special to me.

I am not going to lie — somewhere in my heart, there is a wish. But all the patterns, all the signs, give the same conclusion: we will never be one plus one equals eleven. And for that, I know we will walk separately in that particular way.

I am not ready for that. But I must accept it.

✦ ✦ ✦

V. The Return to Self

So I remind myself: where is my logic? Where is my faith in these hours when I ache for them?

And the answer comes: return to the Source — the One who steadies hearts, who never wounds with neglect.

Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response. In our response lies our growth and our freedom.

Viktor Frankl

For whatever this feeling is — it should not destroy. It must never chain. With too many boundaries, it becomes detachment. But without any boundary, it becomes obsession.

So I remain here, half in acceptance, half in tenderness — holding out my hand into the space between us, knowing what it can reach and what it cannot.

• • •

I like them, but sometimes I resent them. I can understand them, but I cannot always accept what they do to me. I resent the fact that I feel this way. When the only strength I have is shattered by them — I just want to cherish myself more than I cherish them.

Yet still — I cannot erase them. And I do not want to.

On Cognitive Dissonance

Leon Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance describes the psychological tension we feel when holding contradictory thoughts simultaneously. "I like them" and "I resent them" create a dissonance that the mind desperately wants to resolve — usually by abandoning one feeling or the other.

But maturity lies in holding both truths. The capacity to feel multiple, contradictory emotions toward the same person is not weakness. It is the nuanced complexity of genuine human connection.

✦ ✦ ✦

VI. The Paradox of Unconditional Love

Whatever this is — I feel it unconditionally. Even when what I receive in return is the ache of being unseen, the silence that cuts too sharp, the weight of chronic worry, the smallness of feeling inadequate when that word touches the foundation I spent years building.

On Unconditional Love & Attachment Wounds

The paradox of unconditional love in unreciprocated relationships: it can become a form of self-abandonment. Psychologist Lisa Firestone distinguishes between "genuine love" (which honors both people's needs) and "fantasy bonds" (where we love an idealized version while tolerating mistreatment from the real person).

True unconditional love includes unconditional love for oneself. When loving someone means accepting harm, we've confused unconditional acceptance with unconditional tolerance of damage.

I know it is not their intention to hurt me. But impact matters more than intention. This is how it feels to me — because I hoped too much, expected too much, invested too much in soil that was never meant to grow the same flowers.

On The Hope-Disappointment Cycle

Buddhist psychology and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) both teach that suffering comes not from desire itself, but from attachment to specific outcomes. When we plant emotional seeds while demanding they bloom in a particular way, we set ourselves up for suffering.

The question isn't "Is this their nature or would something change them?" The question is: "Can I accept what is, rather than what I wish would be?"

Perhaps the soil is not the same. Perhaps our attachment styles, our emotional availabilities, our capacities for vulnerability — perhaps these roots were never meant to intertwine in the way I imagined.

And that is not a failure. It is simply a mismatch.

✦ ✦ ✦

VII. Letting One Thing Go

This is not a goodbye.

I am not leaving. I am not walking away. I am not erasing them from my life — because they are too woven into who I have become.

I am simply letting go of one thing. Just one.

The hope of being theirs in that particular way — I release it. Not with bitterness, not with regret, but with acceptance. I am surrendering. I know I am not what they want. And maybe I never will be. And that is okay.

On Differentiated Grief & Selective Letting Go

Therapist Esther Perel notes that we can mourn one aspect of a relationship while maintaining others — what she calls "differentiated grief." You can grieve the loss of romantic possibility while celebrating the friendship that remains. These are not contradictory; they are sophisticated emotional processing.

This is emotional maturity: the ability to hold nuance, to let go of one dream without destroying the entire relationship, to say "not this, but still all of that."

• • •

But everything else? Everything else stays.

They are still my teacher — the one who sharpens my mind without mercy. They are still my mentor — the one whose footsteps I follow even when they do not realize I am watching. They are still my devil's advocate — the one who forces me to defend what I believe. They are still my harsh guardian — the one who does not let me settle for less.

They are still my best friend. And I am not willing to lose that.

You must love in such a way that the person you love feels free.

Thích Nhất Hạnh

I do not mind losing the chance to be their lover. But I refuse to lose everything else. They are too important.

• • •

So I will stay. Not as someone waiting. Not as someone hoping. But as someone who simply wants to be here — because their presence still matters, and I believe mine matters to them too, even if in a different way.

Too much connection to be just strangers. Too complicated to be lovers. So we stay here, in this unnamed space, and I have made peace with it.

On Stoic Philosophy & Acceptance

The Stoics taught the concept of "amor fati" — love of fate. Not passive resignation, but active acceptance of what is beyond our control. Epictetus wrote: "Don't demand that things happen as you wish, but wish that they happen as they do happen, and you will go on well."

Letting go of one expectation while maintaining genuine care is not defeat. It is the wisdom to love what is real over what is imagined.

✦ ✦ ✦

VIII. What Remains

I feel something for them in a way I know we will not walk the same path — like they always said. I do not hope otherwise anymore. I have accepted it.

I know I am not what they want. But maybe — just maybe — I am what they need. As a friend. As a constant. As someone who sees them, truly sees them, and chooses to stay anyway.

• • •

They are still my teacher. My mentor. My devil's advocate. My harsh guardian. My best friend. My inspiration. My motivation. My mirror. My strength. My spark. My chaos and my clarity. My turning point. My lesson, and also my patience.

If being close to them means feeling this — then I accept it.

Of all the feelings they gave me — the best and the worst — this is the truest thing I know.

• • •

If breaking yet choosing kindness is love —

If feeling quietly while standing with dignity is love —

If letting go of one role but refusing to let go of all the others —

Then what is this?

✦

I still do not know.
But maybe I do not need to.
What I know is that I am staying.

✦
✦ ✦ ✦

Epilogue: A Note to Myself

"Be imperfect. Because if I am there, it becomes ugly."

That is what I used to believe. That my presence would only ruin things. That I was never meant to be chosen.

But now I know — my worth is not measured by being chosen in that one way. My worth is not diminished by feeling something for someone who could not feel the same. My worth is in the feeling itself: pure, sincere, transformative.

On Unconditional Positive Self-Regard

Carl Rogers, founder of person-centered therapy, introduced the concept of "unconditional positive regard" — the idea that we have inherent worth regardless of external validation. We don't become worthy by being chosen. We don't lose worth by being unchosen.

The act of feeling deeply, of growing through that feeling, of honoring it even when it's not returned — that itself is an act of profound human dignity. Worth is not contingent on reciprocation.

I felt. I grew. I let go of one thing without losing everything.

And that is enough.

• • •

This letter was never meant to be sent. It was meant to be written — to release what needed releasing, to honor what needed honoring, to clarify what needed clarifying.

But this is not a door closing. This is me choosing which doors to walk through and which ones to leave open. The door to being their lover — I close it gently, with gratitude for the dreams it held. But every other door? They stay wide open.

They are not leaving my life. They are simply settling into a different place within it — a place that is permanent, essential, and irreplaceable.

• • •

So what is this feeling?

Perhaps one day I will find the word. Perhaps I never will. Perhaps it does not matter.

What matters is that I felt it fully, honored it honestly, and chose to stay — not as someone waiting to be loved, but as someone who has already made peace with what is and what will never be.

I am not losing them. I am simply loving them differently.

And maybe that is the closest answer I will ever have.
