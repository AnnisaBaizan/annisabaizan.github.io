On Naming What We Feel

There is a particular discomfort in feeling something that has no clean name — not quite love, not just care, not simply admiration. Psychologists call this alexithymia-adjacent ambiguity: when the emotion is real and strong, but the vocabulary does not exist to contain it.

The inability to name a feeling does not make it less true. Sometimes the unnamed ones are the deepest.

On Being a Safe Harbor vs. a Home

There is a painful asymmetry in some relationships: one person becomes where the other goes to breathe, to cry, to land — but never the person they come home to. The one who holds space becomes invisible in their constancy.

Being a safe harbor is valuable. But the harbor deserves to be seen, not just used. If you find yourself always receiving someone else's wreckage while carrying your own alone — that asymmetry is worth naming.

On Unrequited Feeling as a Growth Catalyst

There is a version of unrequited love that quietly transforms the person who feels it — not because they are trying to be chosen, but because caring deeply about someone makes you want to be worthy of the encounter. You sharpen yourself. You learn things you otherwise wouldn't.

This is not delusion. This is one of the most sincere forms of growth: becoming better not for approval, but out of genuine respect for who the other person is.

On Feeling vs. Attachment

There is a crucial distinction between feeling something and being attached to an outcome. A feeling can be pure and real without requiring the other person to reciprocate. Attachment is where the suffering lives — in the clinging, the hoping, the measuring of worth against another's response.

Learning to feel fully without attaching to what you want in return is one of the harder practices in love. But it is also one of the most freeing.

VII

This is not a goodbye.

I am not leaving. I am not walking away. I am not erasing you from my life — because you are too woven into who I have become.

I am simply letting go of one thing. Just one.

The hope of being yours in that particular way — I release it. Not with bitterness, not with regret, but with acceptance. I am surrendering. I know I am not what you want. And maybe I never will be. And that is okay.

So I will stay. Not as someone waiting. Not as someone hoping. But as someone who simply wants to be here — because your presence still matters, and I believe mine matters to you too, even if in a different way.

On Precision in Letting Go

There is a difference between letting go of a person and letting go of a role you hoped to have with them. The first is loss. The second is clarity. When you can name exactly what you are releasing — not "everything," but this one specific hope — the grief becomes smaller, more manageable, more honest.

"Letting go" is not always a door closing. Sometimes it is simply choosing which door you no longer need to stand in front of.
